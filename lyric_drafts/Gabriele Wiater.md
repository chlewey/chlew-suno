# Gabriele Wiater — Biography

---

## Origins

Gabriele Wiater was born on 16 March 1989 in Potsdam, in the German Democratic Republic, the middle child of three. Her older brother Matthias was born in 1985; her younger brother Lars in 1991. Her mother was Potsdam-born, rooted in the city across generations. Her father came from elsewhere — his family was not from Potsdam, and Gabi never developed a meaningful bond with that side. He had settled in the city through work, eventually as a machinist at one of the VEB factories that formed the backbone of the GDR's industrial economy and would become its most visible casualty after reunification.

The Potsdam she grew up in was the strange suspended aftermath of that collapse — a working-class community whose economic logic had been removed almost overnight. The VEB factories were closing or had closed. Her father's machinist skills, valuable in one system, became precarious in another. The social structures that had organized daily life under the GDR disappeared without adequate replacement. For a family like the Wiaters, the nineties were a decade of adjustment that never quite resolved into stability.

Matthias, the eldest, was four when the Wall fell, a teenager through the worst of the economic dislocation. He began using drugs in his teens, fell into bad company, had his first encounter with the law as a minor. He kept the habit into adulthood, the associations around it hardening rather than loosening, and eventually served three years in prison for theft. He was, throughout this, never excluded from his mother's house or her love — a fact Gabi would return to decades later, in a philosophical essay, as evidence that love does not function as a finite resource.

Lars, born two years after Gabi, found a cleaner path. He would eventually qualify as an electrical engineer, settle in Berlin with his wife, and raise two sons — Magnus, born 2016, and Philip, born 2019. By Gabi's own account he is probably the most settled of the three, and almost certainly their mother's favorite, a preference that she observed without bitterness and that never required the others to receive less.

---

## Her Father, and the Workshop

Whatever the tensions of the Wiater household, Gabi's relationship with her father was the closest she had to an uncomplicated bond — not frictionless, but real in the way that mattered. He was a practical man who understood material and process and recognized what his daughter was doing without having an art-world name for it. The drawings she made as a child were detailed studies of industrial equipment, cranes along the Havel, portraits of workers. He encouraged the habit without romanticizing it.

When she was sixteen he steered her toward an Ausbildung — a vocational qualification in industrial welding, practical and certifiable, a skill that could be held in the hand. It was his idea, not hers, and she understood it as such. She entered the program in 2006 and completed it by 2009, earning her certification at nineteen or twenty. What her father probably intended as a foundation she absorbed as a language. Evenings in the family garage, she began treating the MIG torch as an extension of drawing — producing small steel maquettes alongside oil paintings that layered the industrial architecture of her daily life with portraits of the people around her: her father, Matthias, the workers she observed along the river. The dual register — technical precision and painterly attention — would define everything she made afterward.

---

## The Friction With Her Mother, and the Leaving

Adolescence sharpened what had always been structural. Her mother was not a warm woman in any conventional sense — her affection dry, almost brittle, directed at what she understood as her children's best interests rather than what her children understood as their own desires. The fights of Gabi's teenage years were the ordinary kind and the kind that cut deeper: household responsibilities, friends her mother didn't approve of, boyfriends, and, increasingly, the artistic direction Gabi was pursuing with a seriousness her mother could not recognize as practical or safe.

The friction was not hatred, though it sometimes produced things that resembled it. Both of them said terrible things. The love persisted underneath all of it without interruption — but that continuity was not visible from inside the arguments. What was visible, to a young woman of seventeen, eighteen, nineteen, was a mother who could not see what she was becoming and a household that felt like a room she already knew every corner of.

The welding certification in 2009 was, among other things, a credential that made leaving possible. She had a skill. She had a direction. She had New York. In August 2009, on an F-1 student visa, she arrived. She was twenty years old.

---

## New York: The First Years

NYU Steinhardt enrolled her in Media, Culture, and Communication — a degree that would teach her how images circulate, how messages are constructed and received, how the machinery of cultural production operates beneath its surfaces. The early years were, by her own description, almost monastic in their focus. The loneliness was real. The city was enormous and she knew almost no one in it. Her response was the response of someone who had learned to convert feeling into labor: she worked.

Between 2010 and 2012, summers meant returning to Germany — to Potsdam specifically, though not in any way that required sustained contact with her mother. Her father was there, and while the relationship was not without its friction, it was the connection she wanted. She could lose herself in the workshop, in the making, in the physical presence of the man who had first handed her a torch and understood what she did with it. Her mother was in the same city, in the same house, but Gabi managed the proximity by being consumed with work — a strategy that preserved the peace without requiring reconciliation. The summers of 2010, 2011, and the first part of 2012 followed this pattern.

In New York between visits, she had found an arrangement that suited the situation exactly. A Polish-American welder in Queens allowed her to use his shop's equipment for her artistic work in exchange for under-the-table labor, paid well below market rate. It was not her first experience of that kind of informal arrangement — she had navigated similar situations before — and it solved the fundamental problem of a young artist with a technically demanding practice and no institutional resources. The shop was not the later studio, but it would prove consequential: the owner, having come to know Gabi and the circle forming around her, would later help her and her friends identify and secure an old factory space in Long Island City that became their shared studio. The arrangement was practical and the relationship was real.

---

## The Death of Her Father

She returned to New York in the summer of 2012 for her final year of study with her father still alive. The news arrived when she was already back — already enrolled, already in the rhythm of the final undergraduate year. A sudden heart attack. He was gone.

She did not go back for the funeral.

The documents don't specify whether this was a matter of finances, visa complications, the timing of the academic year, or some combination of these with the particular paralysis that grief at a distance produces. What is clear is that she didn't return. And what that meant, practically, was that the connection to Potsdam she had carefully maintained through the summers — the one organized around her father and the workshop, the one that allowed her to be in the same city as her mother without having to fully navigate that relationship — was severed at its root. Potsdam without her father was a different place entirely: her mother, with whom nothing had been resolved; Matthias, by then in prison; the streets of a city that now held only what she had been trying to outgrow.

She carried the grief into her final undergraduate year and did not publicly perform it. The paintings from this period registered it in the only language available — the emotional register of the work sharpened in ways she could feel but not yet fully explain.

---

## The Summer of 2013, and Burnout

She graduated from NYU in 2013 having been admitted to the SVA MFA Fine Arts program. The summer between the two was one she spent in New York. The reasoning she gave herself was reasonable: preparation for the MFA, portfolio development, the continuation of the Queens welding arrangement that had been productive for three years. All of this was true. It was also true that Potsdam without her father — with Matthias in prison, with her mother and the unresolved friction and the absence of the mediating figure who had understood her — was not a place she was ready to return to. The summer work was genuine and also a permission structure. She needed to convince herself it was necessary.

By the time she entered SVA in the fall of 2013, something had accumulated that was not simply grief. The years of near-monastic focus, the loneliness she had compensated for rather than addressed, the loss of her father, the summers in Germany now foreclosed, the legal and financial precarity of her position in the city — it had produced a burnout that she did not name as such and did not know how to resolve through any means she had previously used. Work had always been the answer. Work was no longer enough.

What she substituted was not connection but its approximate shape: unattached sex, superficial relationships, the particular social performance of someone who is present everywhere and available to no one. It was effective as a short-term management strategy and corrosive as a way of living.

---

## Mariain

Mariain Schumer entered her life at a group show in 2013, moving through the MFA cohort with the attention of someone who was looking for something specific and knew how to recognize it. She was an art promoter already building her roster, older, positioned, experienced in the particular landscape of vulnerable talented young artists who needed someone to believe in their work. She has claimed, in retrospect, that her initial instinct toward Gabi was to mentor rather than exploit — that pity won out over something smaller in her. The fuller truth appears to be more complicated. She approached with a promoter's eye for opportunity, the particular calculus of someone who had cultivated that kind of relationship before and understood its yield.

What happened instead was not what she planned.

Gabi in late 2013 was a combination Mariain had encountered before — raw talent she could develop, a striving and vulnerable aspirant she could shape — but the process of becoming an anchor for that person transformed the relationship before either of them noticed it shifting. Gabi stabilized, became more selective, more intentional, more capable of defending her own vision. And as that happened, Mariain's role changed from patron to something more genuinely mutual. The predatory calculus she had brought to the initial approach became, almost accidentally, real mentorship, and then real friendship. The transformation was not planned. It produced something neither of them had expected.

The intimacy came later, gradually, as Gabi continued to find her footing. It arrived as a consequence of proximity and trust rather than as a goal either of them had been working toward.

---

## Building the Practice

The SVA years clarified rather than destabilized her. The MFA hothouse — intense, occasionally brutal, full of people who had organized their entire identities around making work — was the right environment for someone with her formation. The industrial welding helped. You could not be precious about process when you had spent years with a torch in your hand as a paying job. The shared studio in Long Island City, secured through the Queens shop owner's knowledge of the building stock, became her primary creative base from 2014 onward — shared with Michael, José, and Susan, a working community that functioned as both infrastructure and social anchor.

Her thesis exhibition Inherited Heat, shown in 2015, drew early critical attention for its fusion of blue-collar technique and post-industrial conceptualism. The title was precise and also private: the heat of the torch, the inheritance of her father's workshop, the grief that had been quietly sharpening her work since 2012 and that she had not yet found another way to name. The O-1 visa — extraordinary ability — was granted the same year, with Mariain instrumental not just in writing the supporting letter but in thinking strategically about how to frame the body of work for a system that needed to be shown what it was looking at.

In 2016 her first solo exhibition, Seam Theory, opened at a New York gallery. Gabi was not present at the Galerie Nagel Draxler exhibition that year — almost certainly an unconscious act of self-sabotage, the kind that announces itself as administrative difficulty and resolves into absence. The paperwork complications she created were probably not deliberate in any conscious sense, but the pattern was recognizable: a version of the same impulse that had kept her from returning to Potsdam, the avoidance organized around a reason that wasn't quite the real reason.

---

## The Mistake, and the Studio

Early 2016: the intimacy with Mariain, which had been functioning on its own terms, was formalized. Gabi moved into the Greenwich Village apartment, began splitting time with Mariain in New Haven. The structural problems were visible in retrospect and probably visible at the time, though neither of them chose to see them clearly. As artist and promoter they occupied adjacent but functionally opposed roles, and in a romantic relationship every professional conversation carried the weight of the whole. The monogamy question — never cleanly resolved — produced jealousy in Mariain that required performing exclusivity neither of them actually wanted. Every boundary in the professional relationship became a potential fracture in the personal one. The fights accumulated. Gabi retreated to Carroll Gardens. They cycled.

November 20, 2016: the clean cut. Which turned out not to be clean.

The month that followed was the low point. Gabi in the Queens studio, alone, not eating, not leaving, the space growing cold as December arrived. Mariain heard through mutual friends and went to her. December 18: they talked, they cried, they were precise for the first time about what had worked between them and what hadn't — no ambiguity, no performance. They slept there that night, fully clothed, jackets on, holding each other against the cold. In the morning Mariain drove her to Carroll Gardens, made breakfast, and they made the kind of promises people make when they are trying to simplify something that is not simple.

Gabi would reference this night four years later, to a teenager she had known for a week, in a single unguarded sentence: *I almost killed myself. Right here, four years ago.* It is the most exposed thing she says in any of the documents.

The friendship recovered. The intimacy continued when it continued. But something had shifted in Gabi's relationship to conflict — she became too careful, stopped pushing back, stopped defending her vision under professional pressure. Mariain, who needed her artists to have friction, found this more damaging than the original rupture. The restoration to something genuine took until the summer of 2018.

---

## The Return to Germany, and Thomas

In August 2018 two things converged: her grandfather's eightieth birthday in Potsdam, and the flight out.

The Potsdam visit was her first return since her father's death six years earlier. Both brothers were there — Matthias, by this point three years sober, married, with his daughter Maria born in 2017; Lars, with his wife and their son Magnus. The family she had left at twenty had reorganized itself in her absence into something she could observe without the freight of the earlier years. Things with her mother were cordially cold — careful, civil, the silences carrying everything the words didn't. There were no scenes, no confrontations, no reconciliation. There were near-silent greetings and cordial hugs and nothing said that needed to be unsaid. Gabi communicated with her mother as she had been doing through the years of distance — minimally, through her brothers, through texts and social media that kept the connection at the thinnest viable gauge. It was not severance. It was not warmth. It was the maintenance of something that neither party yet knew how to repair.

She connected more readily with Matthias and Lars, with their children, with the tangible evidence that the family had found ways to continue. That was enough for now.

The flight out was booked at the last minute through an online posting: a private jet with a spare seat from Teterboro toward Rotterdam, a contribution toward fuel costs requested. She had never flown private. The curiosity was genuine and the timing was right. She boarded and found herself sitting across the aisle from a Colombian man in his mid-forties named Thomas. The conversation started somewhere around New Jersey airspace and did not stop.

When he mentioned he was continuing to Tallinn after Rotterdam, she said yes without deliberating. Three days in a city she hadn't planned to visit. The intimacy that developed felt as natural as the conversation had. When she finally left for Potsdam, Thomas bought her the train ticket and kissed her at the station with the ease of someone who was not performing casualness but simply felt it. She exchanged social media handles and thought: protocol.

He kept writing. By the time he appeared in New York the following summer she had already understood this was something other than what she'd initially categorized it as.

---

## What Thomas Gave Her

He spent time in her studio, walked her neighborhood, was honest from the beginning that exclusivity was not something he could offer. She filed this under: noted, and continued. She brought him to her circle — watched him from across the room, clearly out of his element, entirely unperturbed — and thought: I can work with this.

What Thomas gave her that nothing else had was recognition of the full architecture without requiring any part of it to be different. He saw the structural commitment of the sculpture and the social fluency of the person and found both accurate rather than contradictory. The arrangement he offered — open, honest, built on presence rather than possession — was not a compromise she was making. It was, she would understand gradually and then all at once, the form that suited her nature.

Mariain, watching from the side, saw something she had not seen in Gabi before: not infatuation, but the quality of attention Gabi brought to things she was genuinely serious about. Her observation, rendered privately: Gabi was in love — not with the man exactly, but with herself in relation to him, with the version of herself that surfaced in his presence. It was not a diminishment. It was a description of what it means to be fully seen.

---

## 2020, and the Officialization

The summer escape they had planned dissolved with the lockdowns. Gabi stayed in Carroll Gardens, eventually stopped making the commute to Queens as the city changed around her. She turned to photography — something containable within the apartment — and what began as a practical response became a genuine expansion of her practice, adding documentary intimacy to a vocabulary built on seam, surface, and industrial memory.

Thomas appeared in December, at Teterboro, not alone. A seventeen-year-old from Sardinia named Sylvia Lai was with him — a curfew complication that had become an obligation, he said. There were also divorce papers. Gabi sat with both and felt, in sequence, surprise, something adjacent to jealousy, and then the quality of assessment she had learned to bring to things that required honesty rather than reaction. She observed the dynamic across several days and concluded that the girl was self-possessed, that the arrangement had its own internal logic, and that she could handle it.

On December 23 they drove Sylvia to Newark for a commercial flight home. Then Teterboro, the long flight south. Thomas asked her, without quite asking, to come to Colombia as his official partner. She said yes. Christmas and New Year with his parents, his children, his ex-wife Lucía: a social geometry that should have been strange and was strangely comfortable. January 2021: a full month traveling Colombia together. She returned to New York in February knowing something she hadn't known when she left.

---

## Germany, 2021: The Conversation With Her Mother

Later in 2021, professional commitments brought her to Germany — exhibition work, the arc of her practice expanding into European institutional spaces. She found the occasion to spend time in Potsdam in a way that was different from the 2018 visit. The professional frame gave her permission, or perhaps cover — a reason to be there that wasn't only the relationship with her mother, which made the relationship with her mother approachable.

The conversation that happened was healing in the way that conversations can be when both parties have been waiting long enough to have them without the accumulated pressure of proximity. It did not resolve everything — the years of near-silence, the fights of adolescence, the father's death and the absence from the funeral, the decade of minimal contact maintained through brothers and text messages. But it opened something. The connection, which had never been fully severed, began to carry more than maintenance. It became, slowly, something she could hold without flinching.

---

## The Nomadic Years and the Work

Mid-2021: Thomas took the Upper West Side penthouse, a proper atelier built in, and asked her to give up the Carroll Gardens apartment. She handed over the keys and told herself it was a transition. She was right. She did not yet know what it was a transition toward.

The penthouse was generous and not entirely hers. When Thomas was in it, it was a home. When he wasn't, it felt borrowed. She drifted between there and Mariain's Greenwich Village apartment and the Queens studio, insisting she was fine, keeping her share of the studio as the most independent thing she had — entirely hers regardless of which anchor she was sleeping near.

The work deepened through these years. The survey exhibition Welded Histories traveled from Cottbus to the Queens Museum in 2023–2024. A permanent public sculpture was installed at Hunter's Point South Waterfront Park in New York in 2024. In 2025 she was appointed Assistant Professor at SVA, teaching Line, Seam, Pixel — a cross-disciplinary course that moved between materials and disciplines the way her own practice always had.

In June 2023 she joined Thomas, Lucía, and their children for a road trip from Chicago to Boston, taking copilot turns at the wheel alongside Lucía and Esteban, the trip ending with Thomas, Gabi, and Claire Jones boarding a BBJ for Barcelona. Her first time in the city.

She came back knowing it was the answer to a question she hadn't yet fully formulated.

---

## The Decision

The immigration notice arrived before Christmas 2025. Not a denial — a notice of increased documentation requirements under the current administration's review protocols. She read it, understood what it meant, and said nothing to anyone for three weeks.

She laid out the options the way she laid out a composition before committing. The Mariain marriage — legally possible, practically problematic, the immigration scrutiny predictable. Bogotá — following Thomas into his ecosystem, dissolving the autonomy the arrangement depended on. Potsdam — going home was not the same option it had been at twenty. Berlin — the right practical answer that would feel like defeat, and she was honest with herself about the fact that feeling like defeat was close enough to the same thing to matter. Barcelona — where Thomas had his European base, where Sylvia was studying, where the city's pace and light and art world suited her in ways she had felt on the first visit and not forgotten.

A voluntary surrender of the O-1 was the cleaner exit. It preserved the record. The Queens studio she would hold as long as the finances and the visa allowed. Mariain she would tell in person, over dinner, at the Greenwich Village apartment, because that conversation deserved the weight of the actual place.

She arrived at this not through calculation but the way she arrived at titles for her work — at the end, when she understood what she had made.

---

## Potsdam, April 2026

Two weeks of recalibration before the move, ending on Easter Monday. She visited her mother — now genuinely her mother again, the relationship rebuilt slowly across the years since 2021 into something that could hold real weight. She watched her doing Sudokus, glancing occasionally at the grandchildren around her, and felt what she had described in the essay she was writing in those weeks: surprise at the depth of her own love for this old woman, at the continuity that had persisted through everything that had seemed to interrupt it.

She worked with Matthias on fabrication — sober now for years, married, two children, the earlier life receded into something that had made him and not only damaged him. She walked the streets she had grown up on and felt what Potsdam had always given her: not nostalgia, not resolution, but the particular legibility of a place that makes certain parts of yourself readable.

The essay she wrote in those two weeks worked through the Greek categories of love and found them insufficient — not because love isn't plural but because the categories draw boundaries where experience suggests continuity. At the end of it she listed, without categories, the people she loved: her mother, her grandparents, her brothers, her niece and nephews, Thomas, Mariain, Michael, Sylvia. And, fundamentally, Claire.

She titled it Vor Barcelona. Not because Barcelona was unknown — she had been there — but because Potsdam, revisited, was the place she had to pass through again in order to understand what she was moving toward and why it was a choice rather than a retreat.

She left on Easter Monday.

## Barcelona, first recap

4th July in Queens, with the old gang at the Long Island City Studio.
Just arrived from Kansas City after watching a football match. Real football, not that thing Americans play on a grid iron.
A FIFA World Cup Match between Colombia and Ghana, sitting at Thomas' side.

A couple of days before in Ciudad Punta Espada, attending the inauguration ceremonies. In a couple of days Tokyo, then Sydney. All this at the side of Thomas.
Back to Barcelona by the end of July. Still trying to feel Barcelona as home.

In these few months in Barcelona, Gabi has done some painting, some photography, practice ceramics, but next to no welding.
Not for lack of studio, there is a studio in Poblenou. A project with Mathias in Potsdam. A couple of unfinished structures in Queens that Gabi refused to ship to Barcelona.
Gabi's mind has just been busy in other ways to focus on new sculpting projects in Barcelona.

Sylvia is growing up. Well established in the city, with her own rhythm. Gabi is still making her own networks, with people in Poblenou. Barcelona is still a promise but not yet a home.
And for this hot sumer, the air conditioning in the US and the winter in Sydney could serve her well.

By mid August Gabi is planing a paiting and ceramic exhibition. Syliva and Thomas will be there.
