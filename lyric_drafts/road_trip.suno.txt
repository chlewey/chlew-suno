\chapter{The Chicago–Boston Road Trip}
{\small\qquad 2023-06}

We landed at Midway on the nineteenth. Claire was already there, waiting by the hangar office — calm as always, clipboard in hand. She’d made the right call choosing MDW over O’Hare. Midway’s FBO is quieter, immigration handled within the terminal itself, no need for long taxiways or exposure to the chaos of commercial traffic. The Embraer fit the ramp perfectly. The customs officers barely looked up; Claire’s advance notice had done its work.

From there, we went directly to the hotel in the Loop — two rooms for us, one for the driver. Claire had selected the property herself: discreet, five-star, the kind of place where the staff know when not to look. Lucía and I took the king room, the children shared the double queen. It was an efficient arrangement, practical for the jet lag and the early mornings that followed. Claire stayed nearby in her own suite, though we saw her mostly at breakfast or for brief logistics reviews.

It was, officially, a family trip — Alicia’s delayed quinceañera. The celebration that had never fit the calendar back home turned into this: a long itinerary through places she had only seen in photos. I’d been to both Chicago and Boston before, but never as a tourist. Meetings, collectors, exhibition openings — always schedules and airports. This time was slower, though the clock still ticked behind every stop.

Gabi joined us on the twenty-third, fresh from Bogotá, and with her arrival the group felt balanced. Claire’s last task before leaving for London was to finalize the vehicle — a Pacifica from an executive rental line she trusted. Six seats, quiet engine, unpretentious comfort. She smiled when I saw it: “It fits you.”

The road east began on the twenty-sixth. The driver and I alternated at the wheel, and Esteban claimed the front passenger seat by instinct — his copilot post, maps open, earbuds half in. He never drove, but he had a pilot’s patience, watching every mile marker. Lucía or Gabi took turns riding beside him, while I shifted between driving and resting in the back. Alicia, usually near the window, carried her camera everywhere; half her photos are motion blur and reflections on the glass.

Driving long distances in the U.S. is its own education. The interstates are disciplined — broad, measured — but the rhythm changes once you exit them. Rural highways have a certain kindness, slower traffic and more stories at the gas stations. The landscape feels vast but domesticated: billboards, silos, sudden lakes. Lucía liked the order of it; Gabi preferred the in-betweens, the diners and empty barns.

Our overnights in Erie and Lockport followed a pattern Claire had established long ago: simplicity on the road, comfort in the cities. Two rooms — a king and a double queen — and a single for the driver. The hotels were clean, anonymous, designed for sleep, not memory. Between them came Niagara Falls — Alicia’s request, of course. The mist soaked us all; Gabi laughed, Esteban filmed, Lucía kept hold of the tickets as if the wind might take them. That night we fell asleep early, the air still damp and smelling of rain.

We reached Boston on the twenty-ninth. Claire was already there — she had arrived the night before to prepare the flight arrangements with the crew. The hotel, again her choice, was one of those century-old properties downtown, all marble, brass, and calm. Two main rooms and the driver’s single, same pattern as before. From there we wandered: Salem, Plymouth, Newport, Portsmouth, Portland — short drives, long talks. I began to like the sea again.

When the Embraer and the crew arrived at Logan on the fifth, everything aligned. Logan made sense — customs integrated, good FBO facilities, and clean departure records for those of us traveling on non-resident visas. Claire handled the paperwork; I barely signed anything. She was already in “air mode” again, the same calm authority she has at any airport where Macaw keeps a hangar.

Lucía flew back to Bogotá that day — office duty couldn’t wait. The children stayed with Gabi and me; school holidays bought us time. The next leg would take us south to Punta Espada, then later across the Atlantic to Barcelona. But what I remember most isn’t the sequence — it’s the intervals: the stretches of road when conversation came easily, or not at all; the rhythm of shared quiet, and the strange comfort of watching the country pass by from behind the wheel.
